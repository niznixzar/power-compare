Each dot is one second, or a rolling average taken every second if you choose 3, 10 or 30 s. Across: the average of A and B. Up: A − B, in watts or as a percentage of that average. Samples where either device is under 5 W, such as coasting or stops, are left out. The pink line is the bias (the average difference), and about 95% of dots should fall between the dashed limits of agreement, bias ± 1.96 standard deviations. Longer averages smooth out noise, so the limits get narrower. The green line of best fit shows whether the gap between the devices changes with power: flat means it stays the same.

Both files are reduced to one value per second; samples recorded faster than that are averaged. The shift that best lines up the two power traces is found by correlation, searching on 5-second averages and then refining to the second.

Only the part where the files overlap is compared, and seconds where either file shows no power are trimmed from the start and end. Differences are A minus B, with B as the reference. Mean difference % is the mean difference divided by B's average power. RMSE is the root mean square of the second-by-second differences, and SD of difference is their standard deviation. Coefficient of variation is that SD divided by the average power of both devices. Pearson correlation measures how closely the two traces move together, from 0 to 1.

The numbers at the top use the matched 1-second data. The Bland–Altman and A against B charts can use the 1-second values or 3, 10 or 30 s rolling averages. Averaging smooths out second-to-second noise, so the longer the average, the narrower the limits of agreement.
